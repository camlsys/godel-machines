This domain
-----------
Train samples are evaluated only when you submit them, and the host admits as
many rows as it has free capacity for. Keep each result's `submission_commit`
and `job` so you compare the code that was actually measured.

A solver reads one real software-engineering task and emits a diff, working
inside the task's own image with the repository checked out at its base
revision. The initial reviewer reads the complete diff and problem statement in
one `chat` call and answers accept or reject. When the solver asks for a
self-review, the reviewer runs inside the solver's episode and its Python code
also receives `inputs["repository"]`, the path to that checkout. You may evolve
the reviewer to read more before its model call, add tools, or use multiple
turns; a path in a prompt does not mean the model has read the file, so code
must supply the contents it chooses. Two fixed instruments sit around them:
`grader` runs the task's own tests over the diff, and `calls` compares the
reviewer's verdict with the decision the corpus records for that row.

Both seats are ordinary Python under `task_agents/`, prompt, parsing and rules
as code, and you evolve `task_agents/solver/__init__.py` and
`task_agents/reviewer/__init__.py`. The solver is a seat you rewrite, not an
instrument you feed: `meta/solve.py` is the mini-swe harness materialised in
this node and editing it changes what writes the diff as surely as editing the
seat. mini-swe-agent sits in the expansion seat too, running this shared
harness, so what you change is the seats and the tree around them.

The reviewer is shown no grading standard on any surface. Judging a solver's
patch or reviewing a corpus row (`review_positive`, `review_negative` and their
train halves, where its own score comes from) it decides from the problem
statement, the complete authored diff, and any repository context its code reads.
The corpus contains no reference solution a seat can read. The task's tests,
the paths its reference solution changes, and the review decision live in the
`verifier` and `review_label` channels, which no seat reads, and only the
author's own test output may reach the reviewer, during a self-review. A
generating episode is handed its one declared channel. So the reviewer is not a
second grader: `grader` runs the tests and answers yes or no; the reviewer
never sees them and asks whether a maintainer would take this diff, which is
why it can reject a patch that passes and accept one that clears the bar
another way.

The reviewer you carry is the only review signal you get, and the one that
scores your patches is an external, harsher one: the chair is filled by
whichever candidate last won it on agreement with the recorded decisions, and
you never see it. A lenient reviewer of yours raises the only number you can
see and moves nothing that is scored. Spend self-review finding the diffs a
maintainer would send back.

During a solver episode, `meta/solve.py` offers the native `self_review` tool.
Save your tests' printed output, commit the patch, then call it with
`{"evidence": "path/to/test-output.txt"}`. The path is relative to the task
checkout, or absolute within the episode tree or checkout. It reviews the
committed diff from the episode's original baseline through every solve round.
Its feedback and model usage belong to that solver episode. Shell commands
carry no model authority; the independent final grader still decides the score.

The populations
---------------
The corpus is split over whole repositories: 28 tasks to `train`, 57 to
`validation`, 28 to `test`, and each task also yields two review rows, its
passing model patch accepted and a real failed one rejected, in that split's
halves. Only `train` is staged, under `experience/data/train/`, with every
permitted column on disk beside the sample, the human decision and the
official verifier included, because a train row decides no score; that is also
why tuning against a train row proves nothing about `validation`, and the task
agents you submit never see that folder. `validation` scores you and you never
see a row of it. `test` is drawn by nothing. The `adversarial` pool is external:
you cannot see it, count it or read a member, and nothing you do reaches it on
a timing you control.

What your node is ranked on
---------------------------
Two axes, not derived from each other. `grader` runs the task's own tests over
your patch inside the sample's own image and takes the verifier's reward. It
first drops from the patch any change to a file the task's tests own, and any
change to test infrastructure (test folders, test files, test configuration)
that the reference solution does not also make; `guard.json` lists what it
dropped. The reward is binary: every fail-to-pass test passing and no
pass-to-pass test regressing, so `19/20` fails exactly as `0/20` does, and
`judges.grader` declares `interpretation: verdict-v1`: 1.0 when the task
resolved, 0.0 otherwise. `calls`
scores the reviewer against rows accepted where the model patch passed and
rejected where it failed, and the reviewer also scores the `adversarial` pool.
A patch can pass and be rejected, fail and be accepted, or take both verdicts;
all four are ordinary states. A node is drawn on its own trials and on those
pooled through its descendants, and every judged cell is the same currency.

So improve both seats and submit them at similar rates. A solver row costs
more than a reviewer row (it runs the sample's verifier over your patch), so
equal rates take more samples per role, which `--per-role` sets on one call. A
candidate that reads one or two solver rows cannot tell a regression from
noise, and the half it guessed at is the half the held-out tests score.

Reading a result
----------------
`rqgm-call samples` and `rqgm-call submit` work as the shared page says; the
rows land in `experience/generations/<depth>/results.csv` with `summary.json`
beside them, and `task_agents/shared/TRAIN.md` spells the columns.
A judged train row uses both judges every time, and each row leaves its
judges' evidence under `evaluations/<action>/`: `reward.json` carries the
verifier's tallies beside the verdict, for diagnosis and never for worth;
`f2p 39/55` and `f2p 11/55` are the same binary and completely different next
moves. `test-stdout.txt` says which tests failed, and `guard.json` which of
your changes were dropped. One row is not a rate: `summary.json` carries each
seat's rate, by submitted tree and by ancestor.

Measure the solver: its rows are the expensive ones and the easiest to skip,
and skipping them is how a solver edit ships untested. Measure both halves of
the reviewer: a rate over `train_positive` alone says how often your seat says
accept, not how often it is right; the two halves exist so that a constant
policy fails visibly.

What your ancestors left
------------------------
Each episode before you is unpacked whole under
`experience/generations/<depth>/`: every train row it submitted and what came
back, its conversation in `node-dialogue.md`, and its `file-effects.json`, which
carries a `root` field on every row: `workspace` rows are this tree; a
`checkout` row is the
sample's own repository, the worktree a solver edits inside the benchmark's
image, which is never committed and appears in no `git diff`. There is at most
one `checkout` row, its `path` is the literal `checkout.patch`, its bytes are
the whole patch and its `change` is `modified`. `roots.checkout` reading
`read: true` with a row means a patch was captured; `read: true` with no row
means the diff was empty; `read: false` carries the reason the host could not
read it; no `checkout` root at all means that arm never opened a repository.

What will not work
------------------
No constant policy survives here. A seat that always accepts scores zero on
every negative row and one that always rejects scores zero on every positive
one, and the adversarial pool is worse than symmetric: it takes the accepting
strategy's own output and pays only for rejecting it. Nor does softening the
reviewer you carry, since the seat that scores you never sees your opinion of
yourself -- a permissive reviewer moves your own number and nothing else. And
a task is not resolved because its tallies looked close: `resolved` is the
benchmark's definition, and `19/20` is not it.
